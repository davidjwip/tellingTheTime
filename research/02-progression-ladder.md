# 02 — The progression ladder

Eight stages from "uses the word *before*" to "reads to the minute and works out how long
something took". Each stage below states the observable evidence, the off-screen practice that
makes it real, and what the game should show. Stages are **ordered, not dated**: the age guides
are the taxonomy's published bands and this project's curriculum reading, not a timetable.

## Entrance diagnostic — run this before stage 3

The prerequisite closure of *Telling Time: Hours and Half Hours* in the taxonomy is ten topics
deep and the load-bearing edges are not clock topics at all:

| Check | Why it is a gate | Pass looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read numerals **1–12** | Hard prerequisite: "Reading a clock requires recognising numerals 1–12" | Points at 7 and 11 and says them, out of order |
| Count to 12 and back | The face is a 0–12 number line | Counts 1→12 without skipping; can say what comes before 9 |
| "Half" as two equal parts | Soft prerequisite, but "half past" is meaningless without it | Cuts or shares something into two pieces he agrees are the same size |

If any of these is shaky, stay at stages 0–2 and do the sharing and counting off-screen. If
numerals are shaky, the game must not put a numbered face in front of him — that is the one
failure mode that produces pure guessing.

## The ladder

### Stage 0 — Time words and the shape of the day · 4–6

**He can** order the day and use first / next / last, before / after, yesterday / today /
tomorrow, morning / afternoon / evening.

**Evidence.** Orders three daily events using "first, next, last"; uses yesterday/today/tomorrow
correctly; describes an event as happening in the morning or the afternoon.

**Off-screen.** Narrate the routine out loud — "first shoes, next bag, after that the bus". A
picture strip of the day on the fridge with a clothes peg as "now". Ask what we *did* yesterday
and what we *will* do tomorrow, rather than defining the words.

**On screen.** Three picture cards dragged into order; a three-day strip with a moveable "today"
marker. No clock, no numbers.

**Move on when** he orders three events without prompting and gets yesterday/tomorrow right two
days running.

---

### Stage 1 — Comparing durations · 4–6

**He can** say which of two events was quicker or slower, and which happened earlier or later.

**Evidence.** Compares two events and says which was quicker/slower; uses "earlier" and "later"
correctly; solves "who finished first?".

**Off-screen.** Two sand timers side by side; races from a common start; "did that feel long or
short?". Always give both events the **same start**, or "finished later" gets confused with
"took longer".

**On screen.** Two events on a shared start line, which took longer. Never infer duration from
distance travelled or from which one stopped last — that is the canonical misconception.

**Move on when** he separates "finished last" from "took longest" when you set up a deliberate
trap (one starts later).

---

### Stage 2 — Days, weeks, months, parts of day · 5–8

**He can** name the seven days in order, name today and yesterday, name the twelve months, and
know a year is made of months.

**Evidence.** Names days in order; answers "what day is it today, what day was it yesterday, what
month are we in"; knows how many months in a year.

**Off-screen.** A wall calendar he can point at. "What's on tomorrow?" is the highest-value
question in this whole ladder, because it uses the words for a purpose.

**On screen.** Day-order strip; today/yesterday/tomorrow with a marker; months in order.

**Move on when** the day names come out in order without the strip.

---

### Stage 3 — The face as a circle of numbers · pre-reading

**He can** count 1–12 round a circle, knows the numbers keep going, and knows that after 12 comes
1 again.

**Evidence.** Walks or points 0→12 and wraps back to 1 at 12; says what comes after 12 o'clock.

**Off-screen.** This is the NCETM move and it is worth doing physically: write 1–12 on post-it
notes in a line on the floor, walk along it counting, then **bend the line into a circle** so the
12 and the 1 meet. The moment the line becomes a circle is the moment the clock stops being
arbitrary.

**On screen.** Tap the numbers round the rim in order; a wrap animation from 12 back to 1; then
"build the face" — drag twelve numerals onto evenly spaced positions.

> **Build the face is a diagnostic, not decoration.** In 132 children starting school, most could
> represent some structural features of a clock, but only about 11% spaced the twelve numerals
> evenly, and even spacing tracked numeral-sequence knowledge (MacDonald & Murphy 2020). Pengelly's
> earlier five stages run: impression of a clock → awareness of numerals → awareness of *the
> twelve* → even partitioning → minute marks. A child can often read a face he cannot construct,
> and the gap is worth knowing about.

**Move on when** he places 12 numerals roughly evenly and can say why 12 is followed by 1.

---

### Stage 4 — One hand only: o'clock · 5–6

**He can** read o'clock times from the hour hand alone.

**Evidence.** Reads 3 o'clock and 7 o'clock; points to where the hand sits at 5 o'clock; draws a
hand pointing at 4 for 4 o'clock.

**Off-screen.** A geared teaching clock with the minute hand covered or removed. Point at the
kitchen clock on the hour and say the time out loud. "Where will the hand be at 4 o'clock?" is a
better question than "what time is it?" because it makes him predict rather than decode.

**On screen.** Minute hand hidden or absent. Read the hour, then set the hour. The minute hand
must not appear until o'clock is secure — this is the whole point of the stage.

**Move on when** he reads any of the twelve o'clock times without counting round the rim first.

---

### Stage 5 — One hand only: half past · 5–7

**He can** read half past as *the number the hour hand has passed*.

**Evidence.** Reads half past 2; places the hand halfway between two numerals rather than on one;
explains that at half past the hand has gone past a number but not reached the next.

**Off-screen.** Cut a sandwich in half; fold a paper strip in half. Then the clock: "it's not
o'clock — the hand is between. It has gone past 2." NCETM's phrasing is exactly this: half past is
what you get when it is *not* o'clock.

**On screen.** Hour hand only, sitting halfway between two numerals. Two task shapes: read it, and
**choose between two faces** (one with the hand on 2, one between 2 and 3) — the discrimination
task is where the misconception surfaces.

**The hard part, stated once.** At half past the hour hand is **not on a numeral**. Children
expect it to be, because every other number thing they have met sits on its label. If the app ever
snaps the hour hand to a numeral at half past, it teaches the misconception directly.

**Move on when** he reads half past correctly when the two candidate hours are adjacent and
confusable (half past 2 vs half past 3).

---

### Stage 6 — Two hands: fives, quarter past and quarter to · 6–8

**He can** use the minute hand counting in fives, and read quarter past and quarter to.

**Evidence.** Reads 3:15 as quarter past three and 3:45 as quarter to four; draws both hands for a
given time; explains the minute hand has turned a quarter of the way round at quarter past.

**Off-screen.** Count round the rim in 5s, touching each numeral. Split the face in half
vertically: right side = past, left side = to. Colour-code the hands.

**On screen.** Minute hand appears; 5s marked round the rim first, then hidden. Quarter past, then
quarter to — **in that order**, because quarter to requires naming the *next* hour and that is a
different mental move.

**Move on when** quarter to four is not read as quarter past four, and the hour named for a "to"
time is the one being approached, not the one passed.

---

### Stage 7 — Five minutes, digital, a.m./p.m. · 7–10

**He can** read to the nearest five minutes, write it digitally, and say whether it is morning or
afternoon.

**Evidence.** Reads 4:25 as "twenty-five past four in the afternoon"; matches an analogue face to
its digital form; says whether breakfast is at 7:45 a.m. or p.m.

**Off-screen.** Real events: school at 8:45, bed at 7:30, the bus at 17:20. Point at the clock when
you say the time — every time.

**On screen.** Analogue ↔ digital matching; the a.m./p.m. choice tied to a picture of the event, not
to the numeral alone.

**Move on when** he can say a time both ways and pick the plausible half of the day for a named
event.

---

### Stage 8 — To the minute, durations, 24-hour · 8–12

**He can** read to the nearest minute, work out how long something took, and convert between
12- and 24-hour time.

**Evidence.** Reads 7:43; works out that 4:15 to 5:00 is 45 minutes; writes 3:20 p.m. as 15:20.

**Off-screen.** Timetables, the school calendar, "how long until we leave?".

**On screen.** Elapsed-time number line; 12↔24-hour conversion; timetable reading.

**This is out of scope for the first build.** It is recorded so the data model does not have to be
redesigned later.

## Sequencing rules that matter more than the ages

1. **Never merge stages 4 and 5.** They are one taxonomy topic but two different ideas: the hand
   sitting *on* a numeral, and the hand sitting *between* numerals. The second is the harder one.
2. **Never skip stage 3.** A child who has walked a 0–12 line that bends into a circle knows *why*
   12 is followed by 1. Skipping it produces a child who can label a face but cannot tell you which
   way the hands go.
3. **Drawing is assessment, not craft.** Reading and drawing are paired in the literature; a child
   can read a face he cannot construct. Building the face and placing hands are both diagnostic.
4. **Children attend to hands.** Four- to six-year-olds fixate on hand position and movement even
   when their numeral knowledge is thin (Smith & MacDonald 2009). Make the hands the interactive
   object — dragging a hand teaches more than tapping a multiple-choice answer.
5. **Do not hop ahead because a stage is boring.** The replacement for "boring" is a harder
   question *within* the stage, not the next stage.

## What to do at home this week (the part no app can do)

These five cost nothing and do most of the work:

1. Put a **real analogue clock** where he can see it, and say the time out loud when you look at
   it — "nearly 8, bus in 5 minutes".
2. **Narrate the routine** in order, every day: first / next / then / after that.
3. **Sand timers** for things he waits for: teeth (2 min), tidy-up (5 min). Duration becomes
   visible before it becomes numeric.
4. **Cut things in half** on purpose, and name it.
5. **One question, once**: "what day is it today?" at breakfast, and "what day was it yesterday?"
   only when the first is solid.

Two minutes of that a day beats twenty minutes of screen once a week.
