# 01 — Evidence, readiness, and what clock reading actually demands

What the published research and the three curricula (Hong Kong, England, Australia) say about a
four-year-old learning to read an analogue clock — and therefore what a game for him should and
should not attempt.

## The question this answers

Is a four-year-old able to start learning to read a clock, and if so, at what point does the
clock face itself become the right thing to put in front of him?

## 1. Clock reading is six sub-skills, not one

The colloquial version — "he should learn to read a clock" — names the visible skill at the top
of a stack. Each layer below it can be missing independently, and a game that tests the top of
the stack while a lower layer is missing produces a child who guesses.

| # | Sub-skill | Why it blocks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Time vocabulary** — before/after, first/next/last, yesterday/today/tomorrow, morning/afternoon | Deictic words shift their referent with "now" (tomorrow becomes today); they are learned by use, never by definition |
| 2 | **Sense of duration** — quicker/slower (duration) vs earlier/later (position) | Four-year-olds judge "how long" by how far or how fast something went |
| 3 | **The face as a circle of numbers 1–12, evenly spaced** | Partitioning — the weakest feature in young children's own clock drawings |
| 4 | **The hour hand** — counts in ones, and sits *between* numerals | The single hardest idea on the face |
| 5 | **The minute hand** — counts in fives, so every numeral means two different things | Needs skip-counting in 5s plus a second meaning per numeral |
| 6 | **Face ↔ digital**, then a.m./p.m., then 24-hour | Conventions layered on top of 1–5 |

Treating 4 and 5 as one step is the standard cause of failure. Two hands carry two units, two
directions of counting (past / to) and a moving-versus-jumping asymmetry all at once. The
cognitive-load argument for separating them is set out plainly in Clare Sealy's write-up for
Third Space Learning: working memory holds roughly four items, and "tell the time" quietly asks
for more than that.

## 2. Prevalence — what four-year-olds can actually do

- A synthesis of three American studies: about **10% of four-year-olds** can read an "o'clock"
  time, **45% of five-year-olds**, **all children by seven**; most read to the nearest minute
  **by ten** (Williams 2012, *Image Schemas in Clock-Reading*, as reported in MacDonald & Murphy
  2020).
- Clock reading is described in that literature as "the most exclusive time-related subject in
  primary school, playing a role in nearly every grade" (Burny, Valcke & Desoete 2009).

So: a four-year-old who can read o'clock is in the top decile. That is a reason to teach the
foundations now, not a reason to expect the face to land quickly.

## 3. Instruction timing, not ability, moves that line

The strongest single piece of evidence for starting early:

> Chinese children acquire clock-reading knowledge — for analog and digital clocks — **2 years
> earlier** than their Flemish peers, due to a 2-year earlier exposure. […] the Flemish
> curriculum tends to underestimate what children are capable of.
> — Burny, Valcke, Desoete & Van Luit (2013), *Curriculum sequencing and the acquisition of
> clock-reading skills among Chinese and Flemish children*, IJSME 11:761–785. n = 11,743.

The mechanism is exposure, not aptitude. The paper's conclusion is a criticism of a late
curriculum. Applied here: **starting at four is defensible; expecting mastery at four is not.**
The game should be built so that early exposure is pleasant and low-stakes, not so that it
certifies anything.

## 4. What Hong Kong actually asks for

Read from the EDB documents directly, not inferred from UK material.

**Kindergarten** — *Kindergarten Education Curriculum Guide 2017*: the Early Childhood
Mathematics learning area asks only that children

> develop the concept of time and recognise the regularity of days, months and alternation of
> seasons, and be able to express time

No clock reading. No clock face. Days, months, seasons, and the language of time.

**The 5- to 6-year-old developmental characteristics** (same guide, Appendix 2) do list:

> Able to tell the year, month, day and week. Able to tell the time (e.g. 3 o'clock, 4 o'clock)
> by looking at a clock, **but depending on whether they are given the chance to learn**.

That qualifier is the whole argument for building the game. The EDB is saying this is an
*opportunity* question at five, not a readiness gate. A four-year-old given the chance is doing
what the guide describes a year early — and the guide itself frames that as a function of
opportunity.

**Primary 1, unit 1M4 "Time (I)"** — *Explanatory Notes to Primary Mathematics Curriculum
(Key Stage 1)*:

1. tell time to the hour and half hour
2. recognise hour (h)
3. measure and compare the time intervals in hour
4. solve simple problems related to time intervals
5. seven days in a week, and their names
6. twelve months in a year, and their names
7. recognise calendars

with the explicit remark:

> Students are only required to tell time from analogue clocks using "…o'clock" and
> "half past…". **Drawing hour hands and minute hands to indicate time is not required.**

**Primary 2, unit 2M2 "Time (II)"** — nearest minute, minute as a unit, intervals in minutes,
24 hours in a day, a.m./p.m., "morning/afternoon/noon/midnight", days in each month, leap years.
**Primary 3** — seconds (3M2) and 24-hour time (3M4).

### The consequence nobody expects

**Hong Kong has no quarter-hour and no five-minute rung.** P1 is o'clock and half past; P2 goes
straight to the nearest minute. The UK and Australian four-step ladder — o'clock → half →
quarter → five minutes — is pedagogically sound but is *not* the sequence his school will use.

This changes what the spine of the game should be:

- Build the UK ladder as the **teaching** order, because it is better sequenced and nothing in it
  is wasted.
- But the **arrival target** that matters for school is: read o'clock and half past fluently off
  an analogue face, by P1. That is stage 4–5 in `02-progression-ladder.md`, not stage 7.
- The real hidden prerequisite that school will assume: **reading the numerals 1–12**. The
  taxonomy makes this a *hard* edge into clock reading, and it is testable in thirty seconds.

## 5. Reading a clock ≠ sensing time

These are separate developmental tracks and the game must not confuse them.

- **Word order comes before word meaning.** Preschoolers reliably infer the *ordering* of
  duration words (hour > minute > second) while knowing almost nothing about their absolute
  durations; knowledge of what a minute *is* arrives years later, often only once formal
  definitions are taught (Tillman & Barner, *Learning the language of time*).
- **Duration judgements stay unstable into the primary years.** Classic developmental work finds
  succession and duration judgements unreliable until roughly eight, with children misled by
  speed and distance (Friedman 1978, 1986).

**Design consequence:** never build an estimation task ("does this take about a minute?"). A
four-year-old cannot do it, and the attempt teaches guessing. Order and compare instead — and
where durations must be shown, show them *spatially* (a strip, two sand timers side by side),
because four-year-olds can rank events by duration when the durations are laid out in space.

## 6. What "learned" looks like at each layer

The taxonomy's evidence statements are the most useful thing found for this, because they are
written as observable behaviour rather than as knowledge claims. Two examples, quoted:

> **Ordering Events in Time** [4–8] — evidence: "Order three daily events using 'first', 'next',
> 'last'"; "Use 'yesterday', 'today', 'tomorrow' correctly".
>
> **Telling Time: Hours and Half Hours** [5–8] — evidence: "Read an analogue clock showing 3
> o'clock and one showing half past 7"; "Draw the hands on a blank clock face for half past 2,
> with the minute hand on 6 and the hour hand halfway between 2 and 3".

The full slice, with parent-facing assessment prompts and the prerequisite graph, is in
`03-os-taxonomy-extract.md`.

## 7. Verdict

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| A four-year-old can start learning to read a clock | **Yes**, and the instruction-timing evidence says starting early is the single biggest lever |
| A four-year-old will master it in a few weeks | **No** — top decile at four, ~45% at five |
| The clock face is the right first thing to put on screen | **No** — vocabulary, duration and the face-as-number-line come first |
| The hour hand and minute hand should be taught together | **No** — separate them by at least one tier, and hide the minute hand entirely at first |
| Hong Kong school expects quarter-past and five-minute times | **No** — half past then straight to the nearest minute |
| The thing to check before any of this | **Numerals 1–12**, counting to 12, and "half" as two equal parts |

**One-line answer:** at four the right move is to build the three lower layers properly and let
the face arrive as "a circle of numbers" and then "one hand only". The game should be designed to
be playable and satisfying *at the bottom of the ladder*, with the top of the ladder present but
two tiers away.
